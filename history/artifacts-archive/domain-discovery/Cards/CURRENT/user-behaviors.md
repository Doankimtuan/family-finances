# User Behaviors

## Common Behaviors

- Young families often keep at least one salary bank account with a debit card.
- One partner may own the main credit card because of income proof, bank relationship, or rewards eligibility.
- Couples may share the card in practice without a formal joint-card arrangement.
- Households use credit cards for large e-commerce purchases, appliances, flights, hotels, hospital bills, school payments, and planned installments.
- Families use debit cards and QR transfers interchangeably for everyday payments, depending on merchant acceptance.
- Users often check bank-app notifications more than formal monthly statements.
- Some users pay the full statement balance monthly; others pay only what feels affordable.
- Cashback and points are often mentally treated as discounts, not income.
- Credit limit is sometimes interpreted as available money rather than borrowing capacity.

## Expectations

- Card transactions should appear quickly and clearly.
- Outstanding debt should be understandable before the bill arrives.
- Due dates should be visible and trusted.
- Refunds should reduce what the household thinks it owes.
- Installments should not make the same purchase look like both one large expense and multiple future expenses unless clearly distinguished.
- Partner or supplementary-card spending should be visible enough for household coordination.
- The system should not treat a credit card as cash-on-hand.

## Frustrations

- Statements are hard to reconcile with daily app notifications.
- Pending, posted, refunded, reversed, and disputed transactions can be confusing.
- Minimum payment can be mistaken for the full safe payment.
- Cross-border purchases include FX, markup, and delayed final amount.
- Annual fees, late fees, cash-advance fees, and installment fees appear separately from the purchase that caused them.
- Merchant names on statements may not match the place the family remembers.
- Refunds may be posted after the statement closes.
- Supplementary-card purchases may surprise the primary cardholder.

## Mistakes

- Treating credit limit as extra income.
- Paying after the due date because the user remembers purchase date rather than statement due date.
- Recording a credit-card purchase as money leaving the bank immediately and then recording repayment as another expense.
- Ignoring cash-advance rules.
- Forgetting annual fees or installment conversion fees.
- Assuming a refund cancels a due payment automatically.
- Missing that partial payment can trigger interest on more than the unpaid portion, depending on issuer terms.
- Confusing debit cards, credit cards, ATM cards, and prepaid cards.

## Decision Patterns

- Users choose cards based on annual fee waivers, cashback categories, installment offers, merchant discounts, and approval likelihood.
- Households often reserve credit cards for planned or online expenses rather than all daily spending.
- Users may keep several cards but actively use only one or two.
- A family may stop using a card without formally closing it.
- Users often decide whether to pay in full based on short-term cash flow, not effective interest rate.
- Card adoption can be accelerated by travel, childcare, household setup, medical costs, or appliance purchases.
