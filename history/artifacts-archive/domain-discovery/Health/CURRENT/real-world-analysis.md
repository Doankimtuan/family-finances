# Real-World Analysis

## Vietnam-First Context

In Vietnam, household financial health is shaped by liquidity, employment stability, family obligations, debt, seasonal expenses, healthcare exposure, informal support networks, and the ability of partners to make decisions together.

Public social health insurance is important but does not remove out-of-pocket exposure. Vietnam Social Security describes health insurance benefit levels commonly ranging from 80% to 100% within eligible coverage, with limits and special rules by participant group and care context. WHO describes Vietnam health financing as a mix of public financing, social health insurance, out-of-pocket payments, and private insurance, and notes that out-of-pocket payments can create financial hardship. A 2025 World Bank report states that despite high health insurance coverage, out-of-pocket health spending remains high in Vietnam.

Sources used for health-financing context:

- Vietnam Social Security: medical benefit levels.
- WHO Viet Nam: health financing.
- World Bank: household out-of-pocket health spending in Viet Nam, 2010-2022.
- P4H Network and Commonwealth Fund country profiles for international framing.

## Common Practices

- Households judge health by cash left until salary, not by a formal financial-health model.
- Many families maintain a mix of bank balance, cash, e-wallet balance, and informal family support.
- Savings and emergency buffers may be held in ordinary accounts, cash, gold, term deposits, or simply as "do not touch" mental accounting.
- Debt pressure is often understood as monthly payment burden before total debt burden.
- Medical expenses are often handled reactively through cash, card, borrowing, family help, employer support, public insurance, or private insurance reimbursement.
- Families may prioritize visible obligations such as rent, school fees, loan payments, insurance premiums, and family remittances over abstract long-term savings.
- Partners often disagree on whether the household is "fine" because each person sees different facts.

## Real-World Variations

| Variation | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Salary-stable household | Regular payroll creates predictable rhythm, but shocks still matter. |
| Variable-income household | Freelance, commission, small business, or informal income makes trend and buffer more important. |
| Single-earner household | One income source increases dependency risk. |
| Dual-earner household | More resilience, but also more coordination complexity. |
| Family-supported household | Parents, siblings, or relatives provide emergency fallback or regular support. |
| Family-supporting household | The young household sends money to parents or relatives, reducing available buffer. |
| Debt-heavy household | Monthly obligations dominate liquidity and reduce room for error. |
| Medical-risk household | Pregnancy, young children, chronic illness, elder care, or recent hospital events change risk exposure. |
| Cash-heavy household | Real position may be incomplete unless cash is visible. |
| Digitally fragmented household | Money is split across banks, cards, wallets, and provider apps. |

## Terminology

Common Vietnam terms:

- `suc khoe tai chinh`: financial health.
- `dong tien`: cash flow.
- `du phong`: reserve or buffer.
- `quy khan cap`: emergency fund.
- `chi phi y te`: medical expense.
- `bao hiem y te` / `BHYT`: social health insurance.
- `bao hiem suc khoe`: private health insurance.
- `vien phi`: hospital fee.
- `chi tra tu tien tui`: out-of-pocket payment.
- `dong chi tra`: co-payment.
- `bao lanh vien phi`: direct billing / hospital guarantee.
- `hoan tra bao hiem`: insurance reimbursement.
- `nghia vu hang thang`: monthly obligation.
- `ap luc tai chinh`: financial pressure.

## Industry Standards And Concepts

- Financial-health frameworks commonly consider liquidity, income stability, spending patterns, debt burden, savings behavior, protection against shocks, and confidence/control.
- Household finance products often present a score, level, traffic-light state, pulse, checklist, or risk indicators.
- Health-financing frameworks distinguish public financing, private insurance, and household out-of-pocket payment.
- Insurance coverage is not the same as full protection; eligibility, limits, exclusions, referral rules, network rules, co-payments, and reimbursement timing affect real household exposure.
- Scoring can be useful, but false precision is dangerous when data is incomplete.
- A financial-health signal should be interpreted as a condition estimate, not a guarantee or advice.

## International Differences That Affect Product Design

- Countries differ in open banking access, credit bureau availability, insurance claim data, and healthcare reimbursement practices.
- In countries with high medical out-of-pocket exposure, Health must understand emergency and medical-risk pressure more explicitly.
- In countries with more standardized credit reporting, debt risk may be more externally verifiable.
- In countries with universal healthcare and low out-of-pocket burden, medical shock may be less financially central than housing, employment, or childcare.
