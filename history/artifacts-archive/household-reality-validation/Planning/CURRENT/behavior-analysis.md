# Behavior Analysis

## Common Habits

- Checking bank or wallet balance before spending.
- Mentally subtracting rent, bills, debt, and family support.
- Keeping important money in a separate account, wallet, cash envelope, or term deposit.
- Using chat messages to remember obligations.
- Discussing large purchases before acting.
- Reviewing the month only when money feels tight.
- Treating small cash spending as not worth recording.

## Decision Patterns

- Fixed obligations first.
- Children, housing, and parents often outrank discretionary spending.
- Salary timing matters more emotionally than annual income.
- One partner often acts as operator while both partners care about outcomes.
- Users reduce flexible spending before touching rent, debt, school, or family support.
- Users abandon systems that demand perfect daily maintenance.

## Typical Mistakes

- Mistaking account balance for free-to-spend money.
- Counting expected income before it arrives.
- Forgetting annual, seasonal, school, insurance, or vehicle costs.
- Treating a credit limit as available money.
- Confusing goal progress with actual saved money.
- Letting cash spending disappear.
- Double-counting transfers.
- Avoiding review because it feels stressful.

## Financial Awareness

Most young households understand concrete concepts:

- Salary.
- Rent.
- Bills.
- Debt payment.
- Savings goal.
- Due date.
- Family support.
- Emergency money.

Formal concepts may be less natural:

- Variance.
- Rollover.
- Allocation capacity.
- Zero-based budgeting.
- Sinking fund.
- Virtual movement.

## Emotional Factors

- Planning creates confidence when it makes commitments visible.
- Planning creates anxiety when it exposes shortage.
- Shared planning can create trust when both partners see the same context.
- Shared planning can create tension when it feels like surveillance.
- Corrections can feel like failure unless treated as normal household learning.
- Goal progress creates achievement when users believe the progress is real and protected.

## Manual Work Users Accept

- Entering major recurring bills.
- Creating a few important jars or goals.
- Reviewing large expenses.
- Correcting obvious mistakes.
- Updating salary or rent changes.
- Confirming month-end understanding when value is clear.

## Manual Work Users Hate

- Recording every small purchase forever.
- Categorizing too many tiny items.
- Re-entering information already visible in bank or wallet apps.
- Explaining every partner transaction.
- Maintaining complex category trees.
- Updating stale assumptions that are hard to find.
- Doing month review when it feels like punishment.
