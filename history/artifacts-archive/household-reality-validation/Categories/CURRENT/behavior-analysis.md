# Behavior Analysis

## Common Habits

- Users categorize around visible transactions, not abstract setup.
- Users rely on merchant names and notes more than formal finance terms.
- Users postpone unclear decisions.
- Users use broad categories for small purchases.
- Users care about categories more during month-end review than during busy daily life.
- Users remember emotional or unusual spending better than routine spending.
- Users often discuss unclear shared spending through chat or in person.

## Decision Patterns

- High amount means higher attention.
- Recurring merchant means faster categorization.
- Ambiguous family transfer means higher discussion.
- Cash spending means lower precision.
- Credit-card or wallet statement text means more dependency on provider wording.
- Categories that affect partner trust receive more scrutiny.

## Typical Mistakes

- Treating transfer as income or expense.
- Treating refund as income.
- Categorizing by payment method instead of purpose.
- Creating overlapping categories.
- Categorizing loan repayment as the original purchase.
- Overusing Other.
- Changing category meaning mid-year.
- Ignoring provider misclassification.

## Financial Awareness

Categories improve awareness when they reveal patterns such as dining, delivery, transport, medical expenses, or family support.

They do not by themselves create financial discipline. Awareness depends on whether users believe the labels are accurate enough and whether category meaning is shared.

## Emotional Factors

- Categories can make spending feel visible.
- Visibility can create relief, accountability, defensiveness, guilt, or pride.
- Partner-sensitive categories need careful interpretation.
- Family-support categories can carry duty, care, pressure, and conflict.
- Baby, school, home, and health categories often feel serious and non-negotiable.

## Manual Work Users Accept

Users generally accept:

- Categorizing important transactions.
- Correcting obviously wrong labels.
- Reviewing unclear items in small batches.
- Creating a category when the need repeats.
- Using broad categories for low-value items.

## Manual Work Users Hate

Users generally resist:

- Categorizing every small transaction one by one.
- Choosing from a long list.
- Resolving near-duplicate labels.
- Re-entering meaning the system or household already knew.
- Fixing provider mistakes repeatedly.
- Doing category cleanup with no immediate household payoff.

## Over-Engineering Signals

- Hierarchies too early.
- Too many required labels.
- Category rules that users must understand before daily use.
- Treating categories as budgets.
- Treating external taxonomies as household language.
