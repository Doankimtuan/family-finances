# User Behaviors

## Common Behaviors

- Check account or wallet balances before deciding whether spending is safe.
- Mentally subtract rent, bills, debt payments, and family support from salary.
- Keep separate piles of money by purpose when discipline is needed.
- Delay discretionary purchases until after fixed expenses are paid.
- Share large upcoming payments in chat rather than in a formal budget.
- Use bank statements after the fact to understand what happened.
- Treat cash spending as hard to reconstruct.
- Plan more carefully near payday, before Tet, before school terms, before travel, or during pregnancy and childcare changes.
- Re-plan when income is delayed or an emergency occurs.

## Expectations

- Planning should feel practical, not like accounting homework.
- The household should know what money is safe to spend.
- Partners should see the same broad picture without feeling accused.
- Repeating obligations should not need to be remembered from scratch.
- Goals should feel protected from day-to-day spending.
- The model should allow imperfect data.
- Corrections should be possible without shame.

## Frustrations

- Bank balances look high but are already committed.
- One partner remembers obligations that the other forgets.
- Small QR, wallet, and cash payments scatter across channels.
- Irregular income makes fixed monthly plans feel unrealistic.
- Shared planning can turn into blame.
- Expense categories become too detailed to maintain.
- Savings are raided for emergencies and then guilt appears.
- Month-end review feels late, tedious, or emotionally loaded.

## Mistakes

- Treating available cash as free cash.
- Forgetting annual, quarterly, school, insurance, or vehicle expenses.
- Counting expected income before it arrives.
- Treating credit-card capacity as spending money.
- Confusing a planned jar with money actually reserved in a bank.
- Double-counting transfers between owned accounts.
- Letting subscriptions, loan payments, or family obligations live only in memory.
- Not adjusting the plan after a major life event.

## Decision Patterns

- Fixed obligations first, flexible spending second.
- Child, housing, debt, and parents often outrank discretionary spending.
- Salary timing drives confidence more than annual income.
- Couples often negotiate large purchases, but small purchases remain individual.
- If money feels tight, households reduce discretionary categories before changing debt, rent, or family obligations.
- If the plan is too strict, users abandon it rather than maintain it perfectly.
