# User Behaviors

Young households usually use Together-like concepts without naming them.

## Common Behaviors

- One partner records or remembers more details.
- The household has a shared mental model of "our money" even when accounts are individually owned.
- Couples discuss major purchases but may not discuss every daily purchase.
- Partners send screenshots of transfers, balances, bills, or reminders.
- Monthly salary arrival often triggers planning, debt payment, bill settlement, and family support decisions.
- A household may operate with informal roles: payer, tracker, saver, negotiator, family liaison.
- Partners may join a finance app at different levels of engagement.

## Expectations

- "My partner and I should see the same important household picture."
- "I should know when shared rules change."
- "I should not have to ask repeatedly for basic money status."
- "My personal dignity should be respected even when money is shared."
- "Joining the household should feel like acceptance, not account takeover."
- "The product should remember agreed assumptions better than we do."

## Frustrations

- One partner feels like the only responsible person.
- The other partner feels judged or monitored.
- Screenshots and chat messages become scattered evidence.
- Small reimbursements are forgotten.
- Family support is emotionally sensitive and hard to classify.
- Personal spending boundaries are unclear.
- A changed rule surprises one partner later.
- The household cannot tell whether a number reflects real money, a plan, or a partner's memory.

## Mistakes

- Assuming legal ownership equals household relevance.
- Assuming equal income means equal decision weight.
- Forgetting to tell the partner about a changed recurring bill, loan, or savings decision.
- Inviting the wrong email or shared device account.
- Treating personal accounts as fully shared without explicit agreement.
- Letting one partner's private debt or obligation remain invisible until it affects household cash flow.
- Confusing "can see" with "is responsible for."

## Decision Patterns

- Low-value decisions are often delegated to the active money manager.
- High-value or unusual decisions are discussed together.
- Recurring decisions become household habit.
- Emotionally loaded decisions are delayed.
- A partner with stronger financial confidence may dominate.
- A less-engaged partner may still want veto power over large changes.
- Trust is maintained through predictable visibility, not constant checking.
