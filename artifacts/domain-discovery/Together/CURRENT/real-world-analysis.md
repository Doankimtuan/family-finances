# Real-World Analysis

Together reflects the real-world social structure around household money.

## Vietnam Context

Young Vietnamese households commonly manage money through a mixed model:

- Individual salary accounts.
- Individual e-wallets linked to bank accounts or debit cards.
- QR bank transfers for daily settlement.
- Physical cash for markets, small vendors, elders, or emergency use.
- One partner paying recurring bills and the other transferring contribution money.
- Informal support to parents, siblings, children, or extended family.
- Shared obligations even when legal account ownership remains individual.

Formal joint money structures exist in banking, but ordinary households often use practical arrangements before they use formal joint accounts.

## Common Practices

- One partner becomes the de facto finance operator.
- The other partner expects summary visibility, not every operational detail.
- Couples use chat messages, screenshots, bank notifications, and memory to coordinate.
- Shared spending may be split after the fact.
- Large expenses are usually discussed; small expenses may be trusted by default.
- Some households pool all income; others keep personal spending money separate.
- Family-of-origin support may be treated as non-negotiable, even when it affects the shared budget.

## Real-World Variations

Income model:

- Single income household.
- Dual income household with pooled income.
- Dual income household with fixed monthly contributions.
- Variable income household with one or both partners freelancing or running a small business.

Control model:

- One steward manages nearly everything.
- Both partners actively record and review money.
- One partner pays bills while the other tracks savings.
- Elders influence decisions without being formal household members.

Transparency model:

- Full shared visibility.
- Shared visibility only for household accounts.
- Summary-level trust without transaction-level review.
- Hidden or unspoken personal spending.

## Terminology

Common Vietnamese household language is often relational, not technical:

- "Tien nha" means household/home money.
- "Quy chung" means shared fund.
- "Tien rieng" means personal money.
- "Gop tien" means contributing money.
- "Chuyen khoan" means bank transfer.
- "Vi dien tu" means e-wallet.
- "Noi quy" or "thoa thuan" may describe household rules or agreements.

Product terminology should remain consistent even when real users use informal terms.

## Industry And Regulatory Context

Vietnam payment accounts, cashless payments, and e-wallets are regulated around identified account holders, payment service providers, and licensed intermediary payment services. Decree 52/2024/ND-CP governs non-cash payments and took effect on July 1, 2024. State Bank of Vietnam Circular 17/2024/TT-NHNN governs opening and use of payment accounts, with later amendments including Circular 25/2025/TT-NHNN.

E-wallets are not merely informal balances. They operate under licensed payment infrastructure and are typically tied to identified users and linked payment accounts or cards.

Household financial data is sensitive in practice even when not every field is legally categorized the same way. Vietnam's Law on Personal Data Protection took effect on January 1, 2026, after Decree 13/2023/ND-CP had already established a broad personal-data regime.

## Important Concepts

Household:

- The practical unit that shares money decisions.
- May differ from legal marriage or official residence registration.

Member:

- A person accepted into the shared financial space.
- May have equal practical visibility even if income contribution differs.

Partner:

- A participating adult in shared household finance.
- Not necessarily spouse in the legal sense.

Policy:

- A shared rule or assumption that influences money behavior.
- Can be explicit, implied, remembered, or disputed.

Consent:

- Agreement to join a shared financial view.
- Agreement to expose financial records to another person.

Trust boundary:

- The limit of who can view and act on household financial information.

## International Differences

Internationally, some markets have more common formal joint accounts, household credit products, open banking access, and spouse-linked tax or benefits structures.

These differences affect product design only when they change assumptions about:

- Whether legal account holder and household user are the same person.
- Whether joint account feeds are common.
- Whether one user can authorize another person to view externally sourced bank data.
- Whether multi-household or family-office style structures are normal.
