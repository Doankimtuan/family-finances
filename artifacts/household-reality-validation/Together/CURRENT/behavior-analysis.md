# Behavior Analysis

## Common Habits

- Couples coordinate money in chat apps.
- Screenshots act as informal receipts.
- One partner often pays first and settles later.
- Salary day creates a monthly planning moment.
- Family obligations are discussed separately from normal spending.
- Financial detail is often centralized in the more organized partner.
- The less-engaged partner relies on trust and summaries.

## Decision Patterns

- Small spending is delegated or ignored.
- Large spending is discussed.
- Recurring costs become habit.
- Unusual costs trigger negotiation.
- Family-support decisions carry emotional weight.
- One partner may decide operational details while both expect to share consequences.

## Typical Mistakes

- Assuming both partners remember the same agreement.
- Treating "my account" as automatically excluded from household reality.
- Treating "our money" as automatically giving unlimited visibility.
- Forgetting to communicate policy or recurring-payment changes.
- Confusing contribution amount with fairness.
- Avoiding uncomfortable financial conversations until stress appears.

## Financial Awareness

Awareness varies sharply:

- Some users understand cash flow, debt, savings, and tradeoffs.
- Some users only know whether money feels enough this month.
- Some users avoid detail because it creates anxiety.
- Many users understand concrete examples better than abstract policy language.

## Emotional Factors

- Trust matters more than perfect categorization.
- Shame around debt or family obligations may reduce honesty.
- Pride around income can affect decision balance.
- Fear of judgment can reduce participation.
- Relief comes from shared clarity.
- Resentment appears when one partner carries all invisible labor.

## Manual Work Users Accept

- Naming the household.
- Inviting a partner.
- Confirming important policy choices.
- Reviewing material changes occasionally.
- Correcting wrong invitation or membership state.

## Manual Work Users Hate

- Repeating the same setup.
- Explaining basic household context repeatedly.
- Complex role configuration.
- Granular permission work.
- Manually proving trust after every small action.
- Administrative screens that feel unrelated to money life.

## Validation Summary

Together matches real household behavior if it remains focused on trust boundary, membership, and shared assumptions. It risks mismatch if it becomes either too personal and single-user, or too administrative and enterprise-like.
