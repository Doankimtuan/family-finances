# User Journeys

## Happy Path

1. Household receives or expects income.
2. Partners identify fixed obligations.
3. They assign planned money to purposes.
4. They recognize recurring bills and due dates.
5. Daily spending happens with more confidence.
6. The household reviews the period.
7. Goals, jars, and recurring expectations remain understandable.

Behavioral validation:

- This journey matches households that already budget, use envelopes, or discuss salary allocation.
- It is realistic for medium and high financial maturity households.

## Common Path

1. One partner creates a rough plan.
2. Some recurring obligations are remembered.
3. Cash, wallet, or small QR spending is incomplete.
4. A surprise expense disrupts the plan.
5. The household adjusts informally.
6. Month review happens only if stress or mismatch is visible.

Behavioral validation:

- This is likely the dominant real-world pattern.
- Planning remains useful when it tolerates partial accuracy.

## First-Time Usage

1. User starts with salary, rent, groceries, debt, or savings goal.
2. User may not know whether to think by account, category, jar, or goal.
3. User expects immediate practical clarity.
4. If setup feels like accounting, abandonment risk rises.
5. If the first plan explains committed money, trust rises.

Behavioral validation:

- First-time users understand "what money is for" more naturally than formal budgeting structures.
- Real-versus-virtual language needs validation in ordinary Vietnamese household terms.

## Long-Term Usage

1. Household repeats planning around salary cycles.
2. Recurring obligations become stable.
3. Goals change with life stage.
4. Children, home, vehicle, family support, and savings products increase complexity.
5. Partners rely on historical learning from prior months.
6. Planning becomes a household memory, not just a monthly list.

Behavioral validation:

- The domain becomes more valuable as obligations accumulate.
- Long-term usefulness depends on keeping stale assumptions visible and understandable.

## Recovery Path

1. Income is delayed, bill is higher than expected, or emergency appears.
2. Household realizes the current plan is no longer realistic.
3. Partners discuss what to protect and what to reduce.
4. Planned capacity moves or goals pause.
5. Later review acknowledges the mismatch.
6. Trust is repaired if the plan explains what changed without blame.

Behavioral validation:

- Recovery is central for young households because financial life is rarely stable.
- Strict plans that cannot absorb change are behaviorally fragile.
