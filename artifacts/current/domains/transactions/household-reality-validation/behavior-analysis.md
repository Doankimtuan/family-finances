# Behavior Analysis

## Common Habits

- Checking account or wallet balances more often than reviewing full transaction history.
- Reviewing transactions when a balance feels unexpectedly low.
- Remembering large payments and forgetting small daily purchases.
- Using screenshots, chat messages, and bank notifications as informal records.
- Recording or reviewing expenses in batches.
- Letting one partner handle detail while the other participates only in exceptions.
- Treating the transaction list as a memory aid, not as formal bookkeeping.

## Decision Patterns

- Large and unusual transactions get attention first.
- Repeated small expenses are tolerated until they become visibly large.
- A transaction from an unknown merchant triggers investigation.
- Transfers are interpreted through relationship context, not just money direction.
- Refunds and reimbursements are evaluated by whether they "restore" a prior expense.
- Users accept approximate categorization for low-stakes purchases.
- Users expect higher precision for rent, salary, debt, school, medical, and family support.

## Typical Mistakes

- Treating cash withdrawal as final spending.
- Forgetting cash use after withdrawal.
- Double-counting credit card payment and card purchase.
- Confusing reimbursement with income.
- Recording the same payment manually after it appears in a provider app.
- Mislabeling partner transfers.
- Ignoring bank or wallet fees.
- Forgetting small subscriptions.
- Assuming a pending card authorization is final.

## Financial Awareness

Transactions increase awareness when they answer concrete questions:

- What did we spend on?
- Who paid?
- Which account changed?
- Was this expected?
- Is this shared or personal?
- Did the refund arrive?

Awareness drops when transaction data feels incomplete, cryptic, accusatory, or too tedious to maintain.

## Emotional Factors

- Transaction review can feel empowering when it explains money clearly.
- It can feel stressful when it exposes overspending.
- It can feel accusatory in shared households if one partner's spending is repeatedly surfaced.
- It can feel calming when refunds, bills, and salary are confirmed.
- It can feel shameful when cash leakage or impulse spending becomes visible.

## Manual Work Users Accept

- Recording large cash expenses.
- Adding a note to unusual transactions.
- Reviewing unclear transactions weekly or monthly.
- Categorizing important or recurring transactions.
- Correcting obvious mistakes.
- Confirming a partner explanation for unfamiliar activity.

## Manual Work Users Hate

- Categorizing many repeated small transactions one by one.
- Reconstructing cash spending long after it happened.
- Explaining every personal purchase to a partner.
- Fixing duplicate records.
- Matching refunds manually without clear clues.
- Entering transaction details that already exist in a bank or wallet app.
