# Pain Points

## Current Frustrations

- Spending history without categories becomes hard to interpret.
- Manual categorization becomes repetitive.
- Similar categories create inconsistent reports.
- Cash purchases are remembered poorly.
- Mixed receipts force imperfect categorization.
- Provider descriptions can be unclear or misleading.
- Family transfers can be emotionally and financially ambiguous.
- One partner may carry most of the categorization burden.

## Confusing Concepts

- Category versus jar.
- Category versus budget.
- Category versus account.
- Category versus payment method.
- Household category versus merchant category.
- Income category versus transfer reason.
- Refund versus income.
- Loan repayment versus purchase category.

## Unnecessary Complexity

- Large category lists.
- Deep subcategory trees.
- Formal accounting labels.
- Required precision for small purchases.
- Provider taxonomies exposed as if they were household language.
- Category management detached from actual transaction review.

## Missing Visibility

- Why a transaction has a particular category.
- Whether the category came from user choice, partner choice, provider hint, or prior pattern.
- Whether a category is still used.
- Which categories overlap in meaning.
- How much uncategorized activity remains.
- Whether historical reports changed because category meaning changed.

## Potential Misunderstandings

- Users may think categorized money has been budgeted.
- Users may think a category total is available balance.
- Users may think automatic or imported classification is reliable.
- Users may think all household members use the same meaning for a category.
- Users may think Other is harmless even when it hides important spending.
- Users may think category cleanup changes financial facts.
