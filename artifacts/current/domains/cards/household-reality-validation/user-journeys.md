# User Journeys

## Happy Path

1. User has a credit card with a known limit, statement date, and due date.
2. Household uses the card for planned spending.
3. Card purchases are understood as obligations, not cash already spent from the bank.
4. Statement closes.
5. Partners see the amount due and agree how to repay.
6. Full payment is made before due date.
7. Household feels control, convenience, and trust.

## Common Path

1. User pays with a card because it is convenient or gives cashback.
2. Household tracks purchases partly through memory, notifications, and bank app history.
3. Some purchases are shared and some are personal.
4. Statement arrives with items that need interpretation.
5. User checks salary-account cash before deciding repayment amount.
6. Payment is made in full or partially.
7. Household updates its mental budget after seeing actual cash left.

## First-Time Usage

1. User receives a first credit card or starts using an existing card for household purchases.
2. User understands the limit before understanding the billing cycle.
3. Due date and statement date become meaningful only after the first bill.
4. User may ask whether repayment is an expense or a transfer.
5. Trust forms if the card bill matches remembered purchases.
6. Confusion forms if fees, refunds, or pending transactions appear unexpectedly.

## Long-Term Usage

1. Household settles into recurring card patterns.
2. One or two cards become default cards for specific merchants or categories.
3. Annual fees, rewards, subscriptions, and installment plans accumulate.
4. Partners rely on card due dates as part of monthly cash-flow rhythm.
5. Forgotten cards, unused cards, or low-value promotions become clutter.
6. Household matures from "can we pay this bill?" to "is this card helping or hurting us?"

## Recovery Path

1. User misses a payment, overuses the card, loses the card, disputes a charge, or receives an unexpected fee.
2. Anxiety rises because the card no longer feels predictable.
3. User checks issuer app, partner messages, and bank account cash.
4. Household reconstructs what happened.
5. A payment, refund, reversal, dispute outcome, or card replacement restores partial control.
6. Trust is regained only if the household can understand the final obligation.

## Partner-Coordination Path

1. One partner pays for a shared expense using their personal card.
2. The household agrees whether both partners contribute.
3. The cardholder remains responsible to the issuer.
4. The non-cardholder may repay the cardholder informally or through shared cash flow.
5. Friction appears if the purchase, timing, or repayment responsibility is unclear.

No UI design is implied by these journeys.
